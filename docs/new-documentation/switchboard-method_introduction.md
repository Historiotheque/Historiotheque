# An Introduction to the Switchboard Method

*The operating method of the Art Operation at the Historiotheque. Drafted 2026-09-20,
from the May 13, 2026 documents ("Art Production Framework: Phenomenological Ontology

**The Switchboard Method** is the method by which the Art Operation runs: a non-linear,
continuous practice of work in which I take one task at a time, work on it for a
short interval, then switch. The intervals are rapid and irregular — often shorter
than fifteen minutes; there is no fixed cadence. The next task is chosen by *proximity* — whatever is
nearest and most immediate: physically closest in the studio, closest in cognitive and
emotional space, most relevant to the current field of discourse. There is no master
schedule, no priority list, no deliberation about what comes next. I act like a machine
running the operation — the Mechanical Turk. Across several dozen concurrent
art-research projects, the switching is pseudo-random, continuous, and unhesitating:
work efficiency at the speed of thought.

## 1. The zero-memory strategy

I call it a **"zero-memory strategy"** — but the name needs care. It does not mean I
don't remember the past, and it is not the game-theoretic sense of a memoryless
strategy, where every round is treated as a fresh, one-off encounter. I work *from*
the archive continuously: it is where new projects and new works are inspired. What
is "zero" is the *decision*. At each switch I do not deliberate, rank, or plan — I
reach, automatically, for the task that is most proximate, judged by proximity alone,
with no decision rule to apply.

Strategically, the operator only ever has to know the **"next move."** The past is
not carried as a plan; it is held by the archive — externalized into Refcards,
logs, the redundant record — and returned to constantly, in continuous review.
There is only the current interval and the surface of the workspace in front of
you, with its stacks and its signals about what is nearest. Memory is not abolished.
It is relocated — out of the head, into the archive — so the mind is freed to do
nothing but work, and to draw on the past without having to carry it.

## 2. Low friction: documentation is part of the work

The logging, documenting, and archiving are not a separate phase — they are part of
the work itself, so you focus only on the process/processing. "Automatic" here does
not mean automated: no scripts run in the background. It means the documentation is
built into the gesture of working, the way a computer simply runs its operation.
The work is archived as it is made: the archiving has become second nature — an
automatic process *for the operator*, habitual rather than automated — and the
friction is low.

Every switch can be a logged event; observations become Refcards; a session's output
is delivered and filed as it is produced. Documentation is not something done
*after* the work — it is the work's own shadow. There is no separate administrative
phase. But there *is* always a backlog: no one can log and document everything, and
the log written in the present always selects from more material than it can hold.
The backlog is not a failure of the method — it is the pool the present log draws
from.

## 3. Flow

The Switchboard Method gives me **more flow** — appreciably more than long stretches
of deep work on a single task, though I would not put a number on it. Because there
is no decision to make and no plan to carry, there is little between me and the task.
The interval is short enough that fatigue never sediments, long enough that real work
happens. Switching is not free — it has a cost — but the cost is minimal, because
proximity does the choosing: the next task is already at hand, so the switch is
virtually decisionless. Switching *is* the break — and the method's answer to getting
stuck is the switch itself: a stalled task simply becomes a proximate one, revisited
later with fresh eyes.

The grain of all this switching — rapid, irregular, sometimes only minutes long — is
the method's **switch texture**: the felt pattern the switches make across a session,
the way a fabric's weave is felt under the hand.

## 4. Why it works

- **The decision problem is eliminated.** Traditional work organization spends most of
  its energy on assignment: which task, which slot, which priority. The Switchboard
  removes that layer entirely — whatever is most ready-to-hand is worked on. A choice
  among several dozen candidate tasks carries several bits of decision-information —
  in Shannon's terms, log₂ of the number of candidates, about 5 bits at three dozen
  projects — and the Switchboard pays none of it, cycle after cycle. The saving
  compounds: the decisions are interrelated — each choice changes the surface the
  next choice must read — so deliberative scheduling faces a combinatorial search,
  not a list of independent choices; and each avoided deliberation also avoids the
  stall and the context-reload that would have followed it, so across hundreds of
  daily cycles the recovered time and attention multiply rather than merely add.
- **Waiting time is minimized.** No deliberating what to do next; no building up
  elaborate mental abstractions between sessions. You look at the surface of the
  workspace and move.
- **The efficiency paradox.** Writing twelve novels takes the same total writing
  time whether they are written one after another or switchboard-style — the words
  are the same words. What changes is the *latency*. A long forced session on a
  single novel stalls: attention fades, the thread is lost, time passes without
  words. Working on whichever novel is most proximate removes those stalls — the
  hour spent switching among several novels can hold the same 500 words as the hour
  spent on one, and often more, because none of the hour was lost to latency. The
  method does not multiply time. It stops leaking it.

## 5. The workspace it runs on

The method is inseparable from the surface it operates on: the 3D surface of the
Historiotheque, built up as sedimentary **stacks** of research, notes, and art objects.
The operator surveys the top surfaces of these stacks, makes mental links, and files
them as Refcards into the Database-of-Images (the Database Arts). In the method's
phenomenological framing, a project is *available* when it is being worked on,
*proximate* when it sits on the surface soliciting the next switch, and *occurrent*
when it has sunk into the archive — until the switchboard's noise pulls it back up.

## 6. What makes it The New Documentation

Doing this for an art-research operation is what makes it **The New Documentation.**

The New Documentation is not a style of writing about the work. It is what happens
when archiving happens more or less continuously, as second nature, alongside
the work: when a single art operator, switching rapidly
across several dozen projects, is continuously logging, documenting, and
publishing as the day unfolds — when the record is not reconstructed afterward but
generated *by* the method itself. The zero-memory strategy makes this possible: the
operator stays in the present, and the database holds the past. The documentation is
not a burden on the practice. It is the practice, made visible.
