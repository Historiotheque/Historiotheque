# Cubism as an Art Operation — 26-minute video discourse

## Session

Recorded this afternoon after the walk to the cemetery and the walk to the
library (research stop, books collected), following the two Instagram
walk-and-talk videos logged earlier today. A 26-minute video discourse,
currently converting from MTS to m4p for YouTube upload. (Format note: m4p
may not be the best choice — still to be looked up.) Archiving to
Archive.org planned, method still to be worked out.

## Content

The video takes the concept of the Art Operation about as far as I can take
it right now with my current resources and available knowledge, and tests it
against Cubism: what the records show and what they don't, what the archives
can and cannot show.

We know the innovations — the experiments with perspective, the papier
collé, all of that has been documented. What we do not have is the
day-to-day, hour-to-hour procedure: the experimental methods, the order of
operations, who did what and when. Picasso and Braque kept no timesheets.
People who knew them were naturally privy to some of it, but the process
itself was not logged or documented.

My Art Operation answers this by importing best practices from software
development — a field where the nature of the work forced people to develop
far more optimal methods of logging and documenting than art has ever had.

## Reflection (not in the video)

I have been reflecting on this for months, since taking back up the Cubism
Studies research I worked on for four years starting 2001–2002 (in earnest
and in a more formal way in those years, though it began earlier).

The practices are often inferable — there aren't a million ways to make an
oil painting, and much of it is basic knowledge centuries old. But the
secret sauce is not being documented, most of the time. The question stands:
what have contemporary artists actually done to improve logging,
documenting, and archiving practice? Posting work on Instagram is not an
archive. It is irresponsible to treat it as one — everything can be lost,
and I have seen it happen.

There is a paradox I live inside. I am an independent researcher/scholar: no
institutional infrastructure, nobody doing this for me. I do it all myself —
including publishing online, because if I don't, I don't exist. I have to
log and document everything. And the institutions, the journals, make it
impossible for people with the highest standards of logging and
documentation to publish in them: nothing may have been published anywhere
else, they want exclusivity. So the independent who documents everything is
gate-kept out of the system precisely because they did what the system
forced them to do — more and more exiled from the Promised Land.
