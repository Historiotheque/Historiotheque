## 2026-10-10 13:24 → 15:43 — workspace=[field] × session_type=[research, reflecting] — a research retrospective, conducted by hand
ΔW temp = methodical, recursive, loose

On the afternoon of October 10 I went to the library with some hundred
printed pages — retrospective material, transcripts of my videos,
Experimental Novel material, code — in four folders (four more bought on the
way home, for eight) and spent two hours doing something no screen in my
practice does: a research retrospective by physical arrangement. The pages
were sorted into nested time slices of the work, spread, stacked, restacked.
Shuffling paper is itself an epistemic action: the selection happens by
physical arrangement, whole-brain, computationally very productive in a way
that has no digital equivalent, and I should do it more often.

Out of the sort, three formulations firmed up. The Operator's Three Questions
— *What am I doing? / What am I trying to do (here)? / What am I gonna do?* —
the standing check the sorting kept returning to. A doctrine pair: the method
and the practice are non-linear, but the research *output* must be clear as
day, and it needs the human touch or it reads as machine slop — research runs
to thinking, to operational thinking and doing, to strategic operations, and
the mountains of sorted data are mined for one thing: intelligence, which
feeds strategy. The capstone, in capitals in my notes: INTELLIGENCE IS
PROSPECTIVE. And the retrospectives themselves told their function back to
me: a retrospective tells you the path you have been treading — where have
you been, whither will you go.

Alongside the papers I read in the research-design text I have been working
through, and in Harri Mäcklin's *Going Elsewhere* (Helsinki, 2018), read
alongside Heidegger; Mäcklin is pinned from this session as a citable
phenomenology source for the practice.

## 2026-10-11 — workspace=[lab] × session_type=[discourse, reflecting] — what post-archival means, stated plainly
ΔW temp = lucid, settling, exact

A definition I have owed the record, stated plainly: post-archival really is
*post-archive* — the reality after the collapse of your archives. My archive
strategy under The History-Project and The Archives-Project failed, as I
judge it, and I dismantled the archives myself — the ironic twist. The
post-archival question is how you start over after the collapse, and what
you can salvage from the dismantling. It is not a rebrand; it is a
ruin-and-salvage condition, and the work since has been the salvage. The
story of the failed archives will be told where it belongs, in the novels:
at the end of The Archives-Project and the beginning of The Chronotopium,
the novel where the Archivillus system is built. The failure is narrative
material, kept deliberately. What replaced the failed strategy is the
operation as it now stands — the record kept in the open, in versions, with
the salvage still going on.

## 2026-10-12 — workspace=[lab] × session_type=[research, discourse] — another artist's archive, and the thousand-year question
ΔW temp = dense, lucid, unhurried

The Deep-Archives Project gained its second root. The digital archives of
another artist — some three terabytes, a life's work in files — are in my
keeping, and their care raises the project's questions in their sharpest
form: how do you treat someone else's archives? The curation, the
responsibility, the fidelity owed to work that is not yours. My own digital
archives, for scale, run under a terabyte; everything together is about
four.

From the custodianship, the thousand-year question, which I now take as the
project's own: how do you archive in a personal art operation so that things
last a thousand years? My null hypothesis: data always dies — it has an
expiry date — and the work is against entropy, against the running down of
the universe. Survival is never the default; it has to be actively proven
and maintained, and the custodian's task is the relay: the record preserved
so it can be transmitted from one generation to the next, in high fidelity
and faithfully, the way the blueprints and plans to build a cathedral were
passed on for centuries.

The practical arm settled the same day into a cold-storage plan: the
archives consolidated first on the settled 4TB master drive; then burned,
one disc folder at a time at about 90GB, to archival M-DISC blanks on a
burner that explicitly supports BD-XL and M-DISC — filename hygiene first
(no spaces, shallow nesting), a manifest and checksums on each disc, and
every burn verified after burning. This week the search for the hardware
ended in an order: a 2TB external drive for my own archives, which fit it
with ample room. The burner decision stands open.

## 2026-10-11 or 2026-10-12 — workspace=[lab] × session_type=[archiving] — the night the thumb drive failed
ΔW temp = raw, methodical, tightening

taken up PowerRename, from Microsoft PowerToys, to bring the external
drives' masses of files under proper filenames and metadata, the standards
the cold-storage plan already assumes. The ordering of a life's files is
not housekeeping. It is the archive deciding, name by name, what can be
found again.

## workspace=[lab] × session_type=[research, archiving] — Refmats: seven hundred titles, read as titles
ΔW temp = dense, polyphonic, methodical

The Refmats work — the classification of my own documents under a new
standalone schema, the Refmats Classification Schema — reached its first
full pass in the same night. Two complete file lists were in hand, some 706
entries in total: 705 after the lists' own headers, 704 after one duplicate,
700 classified after four personal files were skipped, 668 documents after
rendition pairing. The pass ran on filenames only — no document was opened;
classifying by the content facets (project, form, source), never by
filesystem data, which is archive-work data and not an input. Then the
topic-level pass, all 700 titles read as titles: 448 placed in
constellations — the schema's four (the document chain, the Work-System
triad, the novel, historiophany) and eight more that emerged from the
titles themselves and stand marked proposed. What the passes honestly
report is also in the catalogues: the source facet tagged on 230 files and
untagged on 470; purpose nearly undecidable from titles (13 of 700);
twenty-two rows at low confidence. The values the passes derived are
proposals. Ratification is mine, and it happens at the library, with the
printed folders — where the schema serves its real purpose: several visits
a week, hours of sorting, thinking, strategizing. Research is the
foundation and the content of this practice; the schema is one of its
instruments.

## 2026-10-13 — workspace=[studio] × session_type=[creating] — texture itself
ΔW temp = loose, glowing, raw

Yesterday, in the studio, I added a few layers of watered-down acrylic to
two canvases in progress, and after the washes I sanded two of them back.
The sanding block I have is too fine for the purpose — perhaps 200 grit; I
am looking at 100 or 120, even 80 with care, because the point is to remove
part of the most recent layers so the older layers show through, without
damaging the canvas itself. It is an old technique of mine, decades old —
thin layers, sanded back, a distressed texture — and it lets the texture of
the canvas itself show through, which is the realization the session left
me with: my work has always been about textures. The texture of the canvas,
of brushstrokes. And the History-Paintings — the series I began in July
2001 — were really about texture: not letting texture show through, but
texture as the subject, a visual interpretation and representation of
texture itself. I have always said that text had texture, and that texture
too was a text — a textile. Text, texture, textile: the three have been one
thread in this practice for twenty-five years, and the sandpaper is where
the thread surfaces this week.

## Decisions

**Decision:** "Post-archival" is defined in my own plain terms and stands as the record's definition: post-archive — the reality after the collapse of the archives; starting over, and salvaging what can be salvaged.
**Reason:** The term had drifted into a label; stated plainly, it names the condition the whole operation answers.

**Decision:** Project charters are written in the first person, and the History-Project charter stands at Version 1.0.0 after its conversion.
**Reason:** A charter is a declaration by the project's own operator; first person is a voice, not a disclosure — the scope keeps the private out, not the pronoun. The conversion changed no content, and versions are assigned at publication.

**Decision:** The History-Project charter is published in the projects folder of the Historiotheque repository, and the projects index is corrected to list the actual projects of the practice, with the charters living there.
**Reason:** The ExperimentalNovel folder of that name belongs to the novel; the project is the larger interdisciplinary thing. The old index listed repositories — containers, not projects.

**Decision:** The Refmats Classification Schema is a new standalone schema, not an amendment of the standing schemas; the values its passes derived are proposals until I ratify them at the library.
**Reason:** The schema classifies my documents in my own idiom; derivation is not ratification, and the ratification instrument is the printed folder, not the screen.

**Decision:** In the public record, the archives that came into my keeping are recorded as another artist's, and collaborators appear by role and not by name.
**Reason:** The custodianship is the record's business; the provenance, and other people's names, are not.

**Decision:** The fall and winter run at the reflective pace stated on October 13 — reading, music, the small canvases, a page a day on the novels; the repositories move to maintenance.
**Reason:** The heavy build is done; ten years in this home is the ground the deep writing needs, and the season's work is depth, not breadth.
